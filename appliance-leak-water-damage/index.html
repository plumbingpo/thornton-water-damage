Appliance Leak Water Damage Help in Thornton

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. The ice maker line on a refrigerator is a thin tube, and it can drip for months behind the unit. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

Our Thornton crew starts by pulling the appliance out and finding where the water went. For a kitchen, the usual places are below the flooring and under the cabinets. Laundry room water goes into the wall at the back of the machine and the neighboring rooms, and with an upstairs laundry, the ceiling underneath. Standing water is extracted, absorbed moisture is measured, and drying equipment is sized to fit the wet area.

Do This First

What to Do Right Now

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Close the Supply ValveBehind a washer, on the wall, are separate hot and cold valves. Dishwashers shut off under the sink. The small valve for an ice maker line sits behind the refrigerator or underneath.
  3. Mop Up What You CanUse towels or a mop on whatever water is within reach, which keeps it from spreading beneath flooring and cabinets. Do not vacuum unless the machine is rated for wet and dry use.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedIf the washer or dishwasher has not drained, leave it shut. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedKeep the part that failed, whether a hose, a fitting or a valve, and get photos of it. Both the appliance maker and your insurer may want to look at it.

What Happens After You Call

After making sure the supply is shut and moving the disconnected appliance, our crew follows the water with meters and a thermal camera into nearby walls, under flooring and beneath cabinets.

Coverage

What About Insurance?

Homeowners policies often cover sudden and accidental events, and a burst hose or a machine overflowing with no warning is that kind of event. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. Slow seepage from a dishwasher or ice maker line is a harder claim, because gradual leaks usually are not covered. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

What It Costs

What Affects the Cost

  • The appliance involved, and if the leak was a slow seep or a fast flood.
  • The time that passed before anyone found the leak, which matters most for slow drips at the back of a unit.
  • Spread of the water into other rooms, and into the ceiling beneath an upstairs laundry if there is one.
  • Any mold growth, because it must be dealt with ahead of drying.

Why It Happens

How It Usually Starts

  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
  • Water under a refrigerator from a blocked defrost drain or a drain pan with a crack in it.
  • A dishwasher that was installed out of level or with a supply connection left loose.
  • Overloading the drum or adding excess detergent, either of which can make the machine overflow.
  • The drain hose of the washer pulling free of the standpipe, or the standpipe backing up from a clog.
What Is at Risk

Where the Damage Spreads

Kitchen Cabinets

From below, a leaking dishwasher wets the toe kicks and the side panels of the cabinets. Panels swell and come apart, and a wet cabinet box can keep the wall behind it damp for weeks.

Downstairs Ceiling Damage

Water from a second floor laundry room drops through the floor and collects on the ceiling below. Sagging, staining and soaked insulation above the drywall are the usual result.

Flooring and Subfloor

A leak runs under laminate, hardwood or vinyl plank flooring with little resistance. The boards swell or cup, while the subfloor below remains wet well after the top looks dry.

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Water standing on a laundry or kitchen floor wicks up into baseboards and the bottom of the drywall. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.
